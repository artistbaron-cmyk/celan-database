# Humor, Laughter, and Playful Speech 1

Status: **Approved for canon and integrated on 2026-09-14**

Purpose: give Celan ordinary words for laughter, humor, jokes, teasing, wit, wordplay, irony, sarcasm, and hostile ridicule while preserving the social difference between laughing with someone and using laughter against them.

Audit baseline: 1,359 app headwords, 1,438 displayed senses, and 808 approved expansion rows before this proposal.

Integrated result: the batch added thirteen universal headwords and one new primitive root, `Hav`, bringing the app to 1,372 headwords and 1,451 displayed senses. It did not add or alter any expression record.

## The actual gap

Celan already contains humor in use:

- `Kavdava ka.` — “No record”: playful denial that an embarrassing event happened;
- `Rinaen welvek an kel.` — “There is traffic in the talk”: a mildly humorous complaint about people talking over one another;
- `Wek'aen aenthael.` — “The song repeats”: weary or teasing recognition of the same excuse or argument;
- `Dar, dar.` — amused or irritated conversational braking;
- `Wek'aen?` — “Again?” whose force can be annoyance, disbelief, or delight;
- `Inko-ka.` — a private matter or joke understood between exactly two people.

The gap is not whether Ohnoshans can produce humor. They already do. The gap is that speakers cannot plainly say *laugh*, *funny*, *joke*, *wit*, *pun*, *irony*, *sarcasm*, or *mockery*. Celan currently performs humor without being able to discuss, invite, evaluate, or repair it.

This matters in ordinary scenes:

- a child asks whether someone is joking;
- a friend says that a story is funny;
- a performer explains that a line is meant as irony;
- a listener says that teasing became hurtful;
- a survivor laughs from tension rather than amusement;
- a teacher identifies a pun;
- a community distinguishes a comedian from a person humiliating someone for an audience.

## Design decision

The proposed system separates four things that English often blurs:

1. **Bodily response:** `Hav` and `Havaen` name laughter and laughing. Laughter does not prove happiness or humor.
2. **Comic frame:** `Dovhav`, `Haveth`, `Dovkel`, and `Dovkelaen` name humor, funniness, jokes, joking, and playful teasing.
3. **Comic craft:** `Keljek`, `Pralhav`, and `Dovhavral` name wordplay, wit, and the practiced comic role.
4. **Social edge:** `Havrath`, `Havrathaen`, `Jekkel`, and `Peljekkel` distinguish ridicule, mockery, irony, and sarcasm.

This is not a claim that every joke is safe or that every laugh is joyful. Intent, target, relationship, and consequence remain visible in the vocabulary.

## Root-reuse and admission review

### Why laughter requires `Hav`

The closest active roots are `Vial`, `Dov`, `Aen`, `Jor`, `Phel`, `Thael`, and `Zhael`.

- `Vial` means joy or happiness. Joy may be quiet, while laughter may come from fear, strain, embarrassment, cruelty, or disbelief. Making laughter a kind of joy would erase those ordinary situations.
- `Dov` means voluntary play or recreation. Play can be physically serious, strategic, silent, or competitive and need not produce laughter. Laughter can also be involuntary and occur outside play.
- `Aen` covers speaking, breathing, and making sound. It can describe the channel through which laughter emerges but cannot distinguish laughter from a gasp, cry, song, warning, cough, or ordinary speech.
- `Jor` supplies a burst or surge but not what kind of bodily or emotional event bursts.
- `Phel` covers warmth, comfort, and soothing ease. A laugh may comfort, but nervous or hostile laughter does not.
- `Thael` covers gentle rhythm or recurring motion. Laughter often has rhythm but is not defined by gentleness or recurrence.
- `Zhael` covers blooming, renewal, soft opening, and empathy. Laughter may open a social moment, but ridicule and shock-laughter make that derivation too morally narrow.

No active root names the embodied response itself. `Hav` is therefore proposed as a new one-syllable primitive. Its center is laughter, not happiness, humor, comedy, friendliness, or vocal sound generally. Approval would authorize only the forms listed here, not an automatic `Hav` family.

The form has a firm onset, open vowel, and voiced release. It remains short enough for spontaneous use and distinct from active headwords and roots found in the assembled dictionary.

### Why humor and jokes reuse `Dov`

`Dov` already centers voluntary play undertaken for enjoyment. Combined with `Hav`, it naturally identifies the comic frame: playful incongruity or expression offered and received as amusing. `Dovhav` does not mean every laugh and does not require a formal performance.

`Dovkel` narrows that frame to one bounded communicative act. It can be a spoken line, a deliberately comic gesture, a visual setup, or a story beat, so long as participants are expected to recognize a nonordinary playful frame. It is not a lie, factual error, oath, promise, or excuse merely because its surface wording is untrue.

`Dovkelaen` permits both undirected joking and directed teasing:

```text
dovkelaen I ser Ya.
“I joke with you.”

dovkelaen I Ya.
“I tease you playfully.”
```

The direct-object construction identifies the person made salient by the joke; it does not grant the speaker sole authority to decide that harm was playful. If the comic act is used to lower or expose the target, `Havrathaen` is the more accurate verb.

### Why wordplay, wit, irony, and sarcasm remain separate

`Keljek` uses the established relation between `Kel` language and `Jek` twisting or turning. It names play produced inside language itself: a double meaning, altered segmentation, sound resemblance, or deliberately redirected phrase. Not every joke is wordplay, and not every clever phrase is a joke.

`Pralhav` names skill rather than intelligence. A person may be perceptive or learned without comic timing; another may be witty without being wise. The term can praise a quick household remark, a worker's perfectly timed comment, or practiced performance craft.

`Jekkel` names a turn between apparent and intended meaning or between expected and actual outcome. It does not mean deception: irony expects the contrast to become available through context, shared knowledge, tone, or events. A false statement designed to remain believed is not `Jekkel` merely because the speaker later claims humor.

`Peljekkel` adds the point or edge of `Pel`. It is irony used to cut, challenge, criticize, or deliver an affectionate sting. It may be warm between intimates or openly hostile in conflict. Sarcasm is therefore socially risky, but it is not identical to ridicule.

### Why ridicule uses `Rath`

`Havrath` does not mean that all laughter creates an enemy. `Rath` marks the adversarial orientation of this particular laugh: the target is placed opposite the laugher and socially reduced, exposed, or isolated. That is the semantic boundary between teasing that preserves relation and mockery that spends the relation for an audience.

`Havrath` can occur among friends, institutions, crowds, rivals, or strangers. A friendly relationship does not automatically make ridicule harmless, and an outsider can sometimes participate in consensual teasing without creating mockery. The word names what the act does in the interaction, not the speaker's private claim about intent.

## Core usage and contrasts

### Laughter is not proof of humor

```text
havaen I ser reth.
“I laugh in uncertainty.”

havaen I ser Ya.
“I laugh with you.”
```

`Havaen` reports the embodied response. It does not tell the listener whether the cause was joy, tension, relief, embarrassment, disbelief, pain, or cruelty. Those meanings require context.

### Funny is a judgment in context

```text
rinaen zhirkel deni haveth.
“This story is funny.”

rinaen dovkel denya haveth.
“That joke by you is funny.”
```

`Haveth` does not declare an object universally or essentially funny to every person. Like many descriptive judgments, it is evaluated within the current participants and setting. An explicit experiencer can be added when disagreement matters.

### Laughing with and laughing against

```text
dovkelaen I ser Ya.
“I joke with you.”

havrathaen La felka.
“They mock the child.”
```

Shared `Ser` keeps the first event relational and mutual. `Havrathaen` marks an adversarial comic act directed at a target. The distinction is social, not merely syntactic: a group can use the grammar of togetherness while still behaving cruelly, and another speaker may correctly name the act as mockery.

### Irony and sarcasm

```text
kes I jekkel an dovkel.
“I notice the irony in the joke.”

aen I peljekkel.
“I speak sarcastically / I use sarcasm.”
```

`Jekkel` can be gentle, tragic, observational, or comic. `Peljekkel` is necessarily pointed, though not necessarily hateful. Neither is automatically a lie.

## Ohnoshan cultural fit

Ohnoshan humor should not sound as if it was invented only for court poetry or ancient ritual. Celan was first stabilized under emergency pressure and then became a native language of households, crews, markets, schools, workshops, barracks, clinics, transit systems, festivals, and performances. Humor would grow wherever people had to survive repetition, fear, embarrassment, hierarchy, boredom, and each other.

The existing expressions already point to three strong Ohnoshan habits:

1. **Deadpan reframing:** an embarrassing event becomes “no record”; a disorganized meeting becomes traffic.
2. **Occupational metaphor:** systems, songs, cargo, archives, weather, and work processes become ordinary comic material.
3. **Relational timing:** `Dar, dar`, `Wek'aen?`, and `Inko-ka` depend on who speaks, who knows the history, and whether the moment belongs to both people.

The new vocabulary supports those habits without prescribing one universal Ohnoshan sense of humor. National voices can shape delivery rather than owning separate meanings:

| National voice | Plausible comic tendency for creator review |
|---|---|
| Mechuman | Deadpan procedural language, system metaphors, and technically exact statements used at socially absurd moments |
| Nivveilian | Sparse timing, pauses, understatement, and one-word responses whose placement carries the joke |
| Zarithan | Direct practical understatement and humor about scarcity, overpreparation, or needless speech |
| Pelagaean | Rhythmic callbacks, shared verbal play, and jokes built through group response or flowing repetition |
| Verdalrisian | Metaphorical reversals, living-world comparisons, and lyrical misdirection |
| Valkeldorian | Blunt endurance humor, affectionate challenge, and teasing whose warmth must be established by relationship |
| Jasaran | Layered meanings, concealed setup, and balance between what is said openly and what the listener is trusted to infer |
| Marakorian | Formal parody, precise imitation, and comic reversal of rules, ranks, or expected ceremonial phrasing |

These are proposed cross-cutting applications, not fixed stereotypes or national ownership claims. All thirteen headwords are proposed as universal Celan with blank national-origin fields.

## Meaning boundaries

| Contrast | Required distinction |
|---|---|
| `Hav` / `Vial` | `Hav` is laughter; `Vial` is joy or happiness. Either can occur without the other. |
| `Hav` / `Dovhav` | `Hav` is an embodied response; `Dovhav` is humor or a comic frame. Nervous laughter may contain no humor. |
| `Dov` / `Dovhav` | `Dov` is play or recreation generally; `Dovhav` is specifically comic or amusing play. |
| `Dovkel` / false statement | A joke is offered within a recoverable playful frame. Deception seeks ordinary belief. |
| `Dovkelaen` / `Havrathaen` | Joking or teasing preserves a playable shared frame; mockery uses the comic act to lower or expose a target. |
| `Keljek` / `Jekkel` | `Keljek` plays with linguistic form or multiple meanings; `Jekkel` contrasts surface meaning or expectation with intended meaning or outcome. |
| `Pralhav` / intelligence | Wit is comic skill, not general intelligence, wisdom, education, or truth. |
| `Dovhavral` / funny person | A comedian has a practiced comic role. Any person may be funny without holding that role. |
| `Jekkel` / `Peljekkel` | Irony is the broader contrast; sarcasm is pointed irony used to sting, challenge, or rebuke. |
| `Peljekkel` / `Havrath` | Sarcasm may criticize without socially lowering a target. Ridicule makes diminishment part of the act. |
| speaker intent / social effect | Calling something `Dovkel` does not overrule a listener or target who accurately experiences and names `Havrath`. |

## Cadence and collision review

- `Hav` has no exact collision in the assembled headword or approved-root inventories.
- `Hav`, `Havaen`, and `Haveth` follow established noun–verb–quality morphology without forcing unrelated senses into an old root.
- `Dovhav` is deliberately compact for a frequent social concept. Optional `Dovahav` smooths the root boundary without creating another lexeme.
- `Dovkel` and `Dovkelaen` remain rhythmically distinct from `Dovath` game and `Dovin` player.
- `Keljek` and `Jekkel` reverse the same roots with different semantic heads: language play versus a turning expression or situation.
- `Jekkel` preserves both written root boundaries. `Jekakel` is the optional euphonic form.
- `Peljekkel` keeps a sharper standard cadence appropriate to pointed speech; `Pelajekkel` is the optional smoother form.
- `Havrath` and `Havrathaen` are audibly related while remaining distinct from ordinary `Rath` conditional usage through their full lexical forms.
- `Dovhavral` is limited to a practiced comic role; approval would not generalize `Ral` into an automatic occupation suffix.

## Deliberately deferred

This first batch does not yet propose separate headwords for:

- a smile or smiling, because smiling belongs to broader facial expression and is not reliably humorous;
- a giggle, chuckle, snicker, cackle, or guffaw, because manner can initially be described with intensity, rhythm, volume, or social context;
- dark or gallows humor, because Ohnoshan post-Fracture use needs creator-led ethical and survivor-context development before lexicalization;
- parody, satire, slapstick, farce, comedic genre, setup, punchline, or callback, which belong in a later performance-and-rhetoric pass if scenes repeatedly require them;
- humorous national expressions, because those should be developed from actual Ohnoshan situations rather than generated as decorative stereotypes.

## Approval units

The proposal can be reviewed in three separable units:

1. **Laughter and humor:** `Hav`, `Havaen`, `Haveth`, and `Dovhav`.
2. **Jokes and comic craft:** `Dovkel`, `Dovkelaen`, `Keljek`, `Pralhav`, and `Dovhavral`.
3. **Social edge and indirect meaning:** `Havrath`, `Havrathaen`, `Jekkel`, and `Peljekkel`.

Approval of `Hav` would authorize only the forms explicitly included in an approved unit, not an automatic derivative family. Discussion or permission to revise the proposal is not canon approval.
