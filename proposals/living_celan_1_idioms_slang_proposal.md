# Living Celan 1: Idioms, Colloquialisms, and Slang

## Proposal report

**Status:** Approved and integrated — Passes 1A, 1B, 2, and 4
**Scope:** Existing canonical expressive forms plus proposed elevated sayings, contemporary everyday idioms, marked colloquial forms, and eight nationally characteristic expressions
**Audit baseline:** 1,350 app headwords and 1,428 displayed dictionary senses  
**User-selected proposal material:** Passes 1A, 1B, 2, and 4; Pass 3 is not selected for the build
**Dictionary effect:** 0 new headwords and 0 replaced senses; expressions are counted separately
**Expressions build:** 107 unique searchable expressions assembled from 38 Living Celan records, 60 expressive lexicon records, and 11 source idioms/proverbs, with two overlapping forms merged
**New primitive roots proposed:** None  
**Review method:** Canon Dictionary audit, active-root reuse, collision review, Ohnoshan Metaphor Test, cadence review, register review, and headword-admission review

## Executive finding

Celan has extensive literal, technical, ceremonial, and world-specific vocabulary, but its lived informal register remains thin relative to its size. The source lexicon contains greetings, interjections, oaths, national insults, and limited market, temple, and battle jargon. It does not yet provide a broad middle layer for teasing, conversational shorthand, indirect criticism, exaggeration, suspicion, reassurance, social alignment, or familiar metaphor.

That gap matters because idiom and slang perform work that ordinary definitions cannot:

- they tell listeners whether a speaker is intimate, casual, irritated, playful, professional, regional, or ceremonial;
- they preserve shared experiences as metaphors;
- they allow speakers to imply more than they state;
- they mark belonging, age, occupation, and cultural influence;
- and they make dialogue sound inhabited rather than assembled.

The recommended solution is not to turn every expression into a dictionary headword. The Expressions build should distinguish these kinds of material:

1. **Established expressive forms** — canonical interjections, expressive discourse particles, nuanced responses, oaths, greetings and farewells, idioms/proverbs, and national slurs already preserved in the lexicon and source notes.
2. **Elevated metaphorical expressions** — formal, ceremonial, literary, or proverb-like speech.
3. **Contemporary idioms** — expressions born from networks, transit, programs, workshops, markets, performance, and other modern Ohnoshan systems.
4. **Colloquial forms** — marked constructions, contractions, omissions, and clipped responses rather than unchanged words with reassigned glosses.
5. **National expressions** — phrases with a universal intelligible core but a characteristic cultural origin.

All proposed figurative meanings arise from established Celan semantic relationships. None depends only on an English idiom, and no new primitive root is required.

For the Expressions build, the eight national slurs belong beside the other socially marked expressions even though they already appear in the Dictionary. They should be cross-listed rather than duplicated or removed from the lexicon. Their offensive function, national target, source provenance, and `Source Ambiguity` review status must remain visible.

## Editorial model

### Idiom

An idiom is a conventional phrase whose social meaning exceeds its literal wording. It may contain ordinary canonical words without creating any new lexical sense for those words.

### Colloquialism

A colloquialism is an informal conventional use, reduction, fragment, or response. Its meaning is often recoverable from context, cadence, and speaker relationship.

### Slang

Slang is a register-marked lexical item or meaning associated with informal social use. It may begin in a nation, generation, occupation, settlement, or traveling community and later spread.

### Proverb

A proverb makes a general claim about conduct or experience. Proverbs should be developed separately from the present batch because they require stronger cultural history and narrative attribution than ordinary idioms.

## Admission and storage decisions

| Material | Recommended home | Headword effect |
|---|---|---:|
| Existing interjections, discourse responses, oaths, greetings, idioms/proverbs, and slurs | First-class Expressions index, cross-linked to their existing source or dictionary entries | 0 |
| Elevated metaphorical expressions | First-class Expressions index with elevated, literary, ceremonial, or proverbial register metadata | 0 |
| Contemporary idioms | First-class Expressions index after approval and form review | 0 |
| Colloquial forms | First-class Expressions index plus register notes; new forms require explicit approval | 0 initially |
| National expressions | First-class Expressions index with cultural voice metadata | 0 |

The Phrase Builder phrasebank is explicitly excluded. It contains useful sentences and prompts, but ordinary phrases do not qualify for the Expressions collection merely because they are stored at phrase level. The Expressions layer needs its own curated data model for conventional meaning, literal wording, register, social use, cultural origin, offense or sensitivity notes, source support, canon status, and review status.

# Pass 1A — Elevated metaphorical expressions

These expressions are viable Celan, but they do not represent neutral everyday speech. Their elemental imagery, parallel structure, and complete metaphorical clauses give them an elevated, deliberate quality. They fit ceremony, literature, public rhetoric, serious counsel, or proverbial quotation. Each row uses `La`, “they” or “that person,” so the proposal contains complete examples rather than grammar placeholders.

| # | Proposed Celan frame | Pronunciation | Literal image | Conventional meaning | Typical situations | Classification |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `Kel La ser mura.` | /kehl lah sehr moo-rah/ | “They speak with smoke.” | To speak evasively, obscure the point, or use many words without giving a usable answer | A public official avoids a question; a friend dodges an uncomfortable subject | Idiom frame |
| 2 | `Vekaen La dren kaveth.` | /veh-kah-ehn lah dren kah-vehth/ | “They carry old water.” | To revive an old grievance or keep carrying an emotional burden after its immediate cause has passed | Restarting a settled argument; refusing to release an old resentment | Idiom frame |
| 3 | `Shen La mura.` | /shehn lah moo-rah/ | “They measure smoke.” | To spend precision, calculation, or argument on something too insubstantial to support it | Debating unknowable details; producing elaborate metrics for a meaningless question | Idiom frame |
| 4 | `Dor La morl an shara.` | /dohr lah mohrl ahn shah-rah/ | “They put a stone on the path.” | To create a deliberate and avoidable obstruction | Bureaucratic delay; sabotaging another person's work; making a simple task harder | Idiom frame |
| 5 | `Rav La zhir.` | /rahv lah zheer/ | “They pull the thread.” | To pursue a cause, implication, inconsistency, or hidden connection until more becomes visible | Investigating a story; following evidence; asking the question that exposes a larger issue | Idiom frame |
| 6 | `Kes La veth.` | /kehs lah vehth/ | “They notice a shadow.” | To suspect an unstated factor without yet claiming proof | Sensing concealed motives; spotting a possible complication | Idiom frame |
| 7 | `Tal La krez an mura.` | /tahl lah krehz ahn moo-rah/ | “They give fire to smoke.” | To intensify a rumor, panic, or weak claim by reacting dramatically to it | Repeating gossip as fact; escalating public fear; rewarding provocation | Idiom frame |
| 8 | `Bel La veth.` | /behl lah vehth/ | “They bind a shadow.” | To attempt to control what is still undefined, hidden, or unknowable | Writing rules before understanding a problem; forcing certainty onto an ambiguous event | Idiom frame |
| 9 | `Rinaen krez ka phel.` | /ree-nah-ehn krehz kah phehl/ | “The fire exists without warmth.” | Something is intense, impressive, or aggressive but offers no care, usefulness, or sustaining value | Empty spectacle; harsh leadership without support; passion without concern | Fixed idiom |
| 10 | `Rinaen esh an kel.` | /ree-nah-ehn ehsh ahn kehl/ | “There is sky in the words.” | The speech is grand, lofty, or ambitious but insufficiently grounded | Overpromising; inflated rhetoric; an impractical plan presented beautifully | Fixed idiom |
| 11 | `Rinaen korin an zhir.` | /ree-nah-ehn koh-reen ahn zheer/ | “A hand is on the thread.” | Someone has active responsibility and is following the matter through time | Confirming task ownership; reassuring a group that a problem is being handled | Fixed idiom |
| 12 | `Ver var dren.` | /vehr vahr dren/ | “The water does not move.” | The conversation, process, negotiation, or relationship is stalled | A meeting repeats itself; a repair is blocked; neither side advances | Fixed idiom |

## Pass 1A metaphor review

- **Smoke (`Mura`)** supports obscurity and insubstantiality because smoke is visible, consequential, and difficult to grasp without being identical to shadow or secrecy.
- **Old water (`dren kaveth`)** supports retained emotion and past burden through the established `Dren` field of flow, change, healing, and emotion. The expression criticizes continued carrying, not memory itself.
- **Stone on a path** combines established obstruction, stability, and journey concepts without making `Morl` generically negative.
- **Thread (`Zhir`)** already encodes connection through time and cause-and-effect. Pulling or holding it naturally extends to investigation and responsibility.
- **Shadow (`Veth`)** indicates an unseen factor or undefined object. It does not automatically mean evil, deception, or guilt.
- **Fire without warmth** distinguishes force and spectacle from care. It preserves `Krez` as morally neutral and `Phel` as comfort or sustaining warmth.
- **Sky in words** develops from `Esh` as height, distance, thought, and perspective. It can praise ambition in neutral contexts; the idiom becomes critical when grounded support is absent.
- **Water not moving** uses the ordinary motion of water as a culturally plausible image for stalled process. It does not change the literal definition of `Dren` or `Var`.

# Pass 1B — Contemporary Ohnoshan idioms

These expressions come from infrastructure and habits that distinguish Ohnosha from a generic ancient or elemental setting. They are the kind of phrases a person could hear in a transit terminal, repair bay, rehearsal space, market queue, apartment, control room, or mixed-national city.

| ID | Proposed expression | Pronunciation | Literal situation | Everyday meaning | Where it plausibly began | Register |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | `Ra belnoraen Ilin?` | /rah behl-nohr-ah-ehn ee-leen/ | “Are we synchronized?” | Are we understanding the same thing, following the same plan, or ready at the same time? | Mechuman systems work and multi-crew coordination; now broadly urban | Neutral contemporary idiom |
| C2 | `Rinaen zheldava ka.` | /ree-nah-ehn zhehl-dah-vah kah/ | “The signal is missing.” | I cannot read your intention; you stopped responding; the expected confirmation never arrived | Communications networks, dispatch, long-distance relationships | Casual observation or complaint |
| C3 | `Var belraku; rinaen la kaveth.` | /vahr behl-rah-koo; ree-nah-ehn lah kah-vehth/ | “The program runs; it is old.” | Someone is repeating an inherited habit, policy, prejudice, or solution after its purpose has expired | Mechuman programmers and institutional workers | Dry criticism; often shortened in conversation to `Belraku kaveth.` |
| C4 | `Rinaen welvek an kel.` | /ree-nah-ehn wehl-vehk ahn kehl/ | “There is traffic in the talk.” | Too many people are speaking across one another for the conversation to move | Transit workers and crowded interregional markets | Mildly humorous meeting-room complaint |
| C5 | `Wek'aen aenthael.` | /wehk-ah-ehn ah-ehn-thah-ehl/ | “The song repeats.” | We are hearing the same excuse, argument, promise, or complaint again | Performers, rehearsal crews, households tired of a familiar dispute | Familiar, weary, sometimes teasing |
| C6 | `Anvekaen Ya mura.` | /ahn-vehk-ah-ehn yah moo-rah/ | “You loaded smoke.” | You brought an empty claim, useless information, or impressive-looking material with no practical substance | Cargo crews, couriers, loaders, and market inspectors | Direct accusation; lighter among coworkers |
| C7 | `Rinaen varvek ka lorvekral.` | /ree-nah-ehn vahr-vehk kah lohr-vehk-rahl/ | “The vehicle is without an operator.” | A project has activity and resources but nobody has accepted responsibility for directing it | Transport crews; later project and civic work | Criticism of absent ownership, not a call for authoritarian control |
| C8 | `Rinaen worzhel an felmek.` | /ree-nah-ehn wohr-zhehl ahn fehl-mehk/ | “The overload is in one component.” | One person or small part of a group is carrying more demand than it can safely sustain | Energy workers, engineers, healers discussing caregiver strain | Serious warning disguised as technical shorthand |
| C9 | `Kavdava ka.` | /kahv-dah-vah kah/ | “No record.” | Playful denial that an embarrassing event happened, or a sharper demand for evidence before accepting a claim | Archives, civic offices, performers after mistakes, friends after a chaotic night | Usually joking; formal use can sound evasive or corrupt |
| C10 | `Rinaen khumkes; ver pralaen La.` | /ree-nah-ehn khoom-kehs; vehr prah-lah-ehn lah/ | “There is an audience; they make nothing.” | Many people are watching, judging, or advising while nobody helps do the work | Public workshops, rehearsals, community repairs, kitchens during large gatherings | Worker's complaint; can invite the onlookers to help |

## Pass 1B metaphor and usage findings

- Synchronization, signal loss, programs, traffic, overload, records, and operators are ordinary parts of Ohnoshan modernity, not imported decorative futurism.
- The phrases extend system behavior into social behavior only where the relationship is direct and observable.
- `Belraku kaveth` is specifically an outdated routine still executing. It does not make age itself contemptible.
- `Worzhel an felmek` warns about dangerous concentration of burden. It should not become a way to blame the person already carrying too much.
- `Kavdava ka` is believable partly because its morality changes by setting: funny between friends, suspicious from an official.
- These idioms are more likely to cross national boundaries through mixed cities, travel, institutions, performance circuits, and shared infrastructure than through ceremonial teaching.

# Pass 2 — Colloquial forms created by clipping and reanalysis

An unchanged canonical word does not become plausible slang merely because an editor gives it another English gloss. The stronger candidates below visibly alter, contract, or leave out part of an established construction. The new social meaning comes from that repeated informal behavior.

| # | Form | Pronunciation | What happens in the scene | Who tends to say it | What the relationship communicates | Recommended treatment |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 13 | `Dar, dar.` | /dahr dahr/ | One person is rushing an explanation, reaching a conclusion too early, or stacking tasks faster than others can answer. Another cuts in with the repeated verb: “Hold on—one thing at a time.” | Friends, coworkers, siblings, teachers, crew leads | Familiar enough to interrupt directly; tone decides whether it is gentle, amused, or irritated | Repetition changes cadence and interaction |
| 14 | `Ilin-ka.` | /ee-leen-kah/ | Standard `Ilin-ka` introduces something possessed by inclusive “us,” as in `Ilin-ka emil`, “our meal.” Informal speakers omit the understood noun and leave the possessive construction hanging: “Ours.” | Crews identifying shared work; households claiming responsibility; communities recognizing a person as one of their own | Inclusion through shared claim or obligation, not ownership of a person | Proposed ellipsis of an existing possessive construction |
| 15 | `Ver-ilin.` | /vehr-ee-leen/ | Standard grammar can negate a noun or pronoun with `Ver`. Informal speech fuses the two into a marked label: “not-us.” | A group repudiating conduct done in its name; a clique excluding someone; survivors rejecting an institution that claims them | Can express ethical disavowal or cruel social exile | Proposed register-marked contraction; requires explicit form approval |
| 16 | `Inko-ka.` | /een-koh-kah/ | The dual possessive is left without its expected noun: “ours-two.” It marks a matter, joke, promise, route, or burden understood to belong specifically between speaker and listener. | Partners, paired workers, siblings, rivals with a private history | This is between the two of us | Proposed dual ellipsis; intimate rather than universally casual |
| 17 | `Dro?` | /droh/ | Instead of immediately asking what is wrong, someone offers ordinary water. The question may literally mean “Water?” while also giving the other person permission to pause, recover, or begin speaking when ready. | Household members, healers, market friends, travelers after exertion | Care without demanding disclosure | Socially conventional omission around an actual offer |
| 18 | `Nor-ka.` | /nohr-kah/ | At an accident, magical backlash, collapse, or damaged system, someone says only the Echo particle: “The actor is unknown or irrelevant right now; work the aftermath.” | Healers, rescue crews, engineers, investigators under immediate pressure | Stop assigning blame until the living problem is contained | Occupational clipping of the existing grammatical particle |
| 19 | `Wek'aen?` | /wehk-ah-ehn/ | The canonical repetition particle is used as an entire question: “Again?” The omitted event is obvious to both speakers. | Coworkers facing a recurring fault; family members hearing a familiar argument; performers asked for another take | Shared recognition of recurrence; annoyance, disbelief, or delight comes from tone | Whole-clause ellipsis using existing Particle |
| 20 | `Athnor.` | /ath-nohr/ | “Already” stands alone when a warning, explanation, request, or piece of gossip has arrived too late. | Fast-moving crews, adolescents resisting repeated instruction, colleagues who have handled the task | I know; it is done; you are behind the moment | Whole-clause ellipsis using existing Particle |

## Pass 2 sense-preservation finding

These forms feel informal because something observable has happened to the language: repetition, fusion, or omission. `Ilin-ka` and `Inko-ka` are not new definitions for bare pronouns; they are possessive constructions reanalyzed after the expected noun repeatedly disappears. `Ver-ilin` is the only genuinely new surface form in this pass and therefore needs direct approval. `Nor-ka`, `Wek'aen`, and `Athnor` keep their canonical functions while standing in for clauses the participants no longer need to say aloud.

# Pass 3 — Slang born inside actual communities

This pass uses existing headwords metaphorically rather than manufacturing a family of compounds. Each item begins in a recognizable social setting. The recommendation is to document these as slang usages first, not to expose eight new dictionary senses immediately.

| # | Existing word used as slang | Pronunciation | Literal canonical meaning | Slang use | Birthplace and scene | Social temperature | Recommended treatment |
|---:|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| 22 | `Mekreth` | /mehk-rehth/ | A system malfunction | A plan, gathering, household morning, or relationship arrangement in which failures keep producing more failures | Workshop language escapes into city speech after technicians repeatedly arrive to “fix” nontechnical chaos | Exasperated but often humorous; criticizes the situation more than a person | Extended informal usage of existing Noun |
| 23 | `Drokav` | /droh-kahv/ | A protected cistern or water reserve | A person who keeps every feeling, plan, or useful detail sealed until directly asked—or even after being asked | Water stewards and caravan households tease private family members: “You are a `Drokav` today.” | Affectionate among intimates; accusatory from an outsider | Metaphorical address use of existing Noun |
| 24 | `Vekmor` | /vehk-mohr/ | Cargo carried as a transport load | A crew member, passenger, or companion who contributes nothing while everyone else carries the work | Vehicle, ship, caravan, and expedition crews | Blunt insult; can also be self-deprecating after injury: “I am `Vekmor` today.” | Crew slang using existing Noun |
| 25 | `Kesmek` | /kehs-mehk/ | A sensor or detecting device | The person who notices every arrival, mood change, mistake, or secret before anyone else | Workshops, crowded households, guard posts, and friend groups | Admiring when vigilance helps; teasing or critical when it becomes nosiness | Nickname use of existing Noun |
| 26 | `Jekkal` | /jehk-kahl/ | A mechanical spring that stores and releases force | A restless person who is visibly holding energy and may leap into action at any moment | Apprentices call an eager child or new recruit a `Jekkal`; veteran crews may use it for someone unable to sit through instructions | Usually affectionate teasing; mildly critical in formal settings | Nickname use of existing Noun |
| 27 | `Kavsul` | /kahv-sool/ | A deliberately preserved reserve or stockpile | The person who somehow has the spare cord, food, patch, charge, medicine, or tool the group suddenly needs | Hearth-Clusters, travel groups, repair teams, and performers packing for the road | Warm compliment; a role earned by preparedness and quiet care | Affectionate role-name using existing Noun |
| 28 | `Drenjor` | /drehn-johr/ | A traveling wave across water | A fashion, song, argument, rumor, or enthusiasm that rises across many people and may pass as quickly as it arrived | Pelagaean performers and traders notice ideas moving between ports; the term spreads through interregional markets | Neutral or amused; dismissive only when the speaker stresses that the wave will pass | Informal social sense of existing Noun |

## Pass 3 metaphor and boundary review

| Slang use | Why it grows naturally inside Celan | Boundary that prevents semantic damage |
|---|---|---|
| `Felnor` | Speakers living with temporal fractures would inevitably compare ordinary missing moments with the real anomaly, especially through youthful dark humor. | The slang must never replace the clinical or hazard meaning, and speakers may avoid it around actual survivors. |
| `Mekreth` | A connected system failing at its intended operation maps naturally onto a plan whose parts frustrate one another. | It describes cascading dysfunction, not every mistake, inconvenience, or disliked event. |
| `Drokav` | A cistern protects what is valuable by limiting access. A guarded person likewise controls emotional or informational access. | Privacy alone is not a flaw; the slang becomes critical only when withholding affects the relationship or task. |
| `Vekmor` | Transport crews already distinguish operators, passengers, and cargo. Calling a capable person cargo accuses them of surrendering agency and burden-sharing. | It should not be applied to children, injured people, evacuees, or anyone who genuinely must be carried except through consensual self-mockery. |
| `Kesmek` | Sensors register changes before others do. Dense households and crews would turn the tool-name into a human nickname. | It does not add consciousness or judgment to the device sense, and it does not mean spy by default. |
| `Jekkal` | A compressed spring makes stored readiness physically visible. The nickname praises energy while teasing poor stillness. | It does not mean unreliable, violent, or childish by itself. |
| `Kavsul` | Shared survival makes the person who preserves small necessities socially recognizable. The reserve becomes a role-name through metonymy. | It praises preparedness, not hoarding; a hoarded resource withheld from need contradicts the compliment. |
| `Drenjor` | Celan already understands water as flow and change. A wave moving through people is a culturally native model of collective attention. | It describes propagation and rise-and-fall, not every popular or important idea. |

## Pass 3 admission finding

These are stronger than the discarded `root + Kel` compounds because each has a speaker community, a reason for being coined, a relationship effect, and a potential misuse. They also create zero new headwords. If later dialogue shows that one slang meaning is stable across contexts and generations, it can undergo a separate additive-sense review under the Ohnoshan Metaphor Test.

# Pass 4 — Nationally characteristic expressions

These expressions have meanings understandable across Ohnosha but arise naturally from a nation's recurring lived experience. National origin indicates characteristic coinage or use, not exclusive ownership.

| 30 | Pelagae | `Va var ser mahr.` | /vah vahr sehr mahhr/ | “Go with the ocean.” | Adapt to changing conditions; stop wasting strength against the larger movement | Navigation, tides, and collective travel | Strong candidate |

| 36 | Marakor | `Va aenaen rathor.` | /vah ah-eh-nah-ehn rah-thor/ | “Hear the past.” | Consult precedent, elders, or remembered consequence before acting | Historical continuity and respect for what earlier choices already taught | Strong candidate |

## Pass 4 boundaries

- The Zarithan phrase praises materially useful action; it must not imply that speech, testimony, planning, or emotional reassurance never matter.
- The Pelagae phrase recommends adaptive movement, not passive surrender.
- The Valkeldor phrase may be admiring or critical. Context determines whether stability has become stubbornness.
- The Verdalris phrase concerns careful attention to living systems. It is not a claim that plants literally speak in every use.
- The Nivveil phrase contrasts perspective with fixation. It does not dismiss small evidence when that evidence is genuinely diagnostic.
- The Mechuma phrase criticizes cosmetic repair and metric manipulation. It does not diminish legitimate temporary containment or emergency stabilization.
- The Jasara phrase recognizes concealment without automatically proving harmful intent.
- The Marakor phrase values precedent without requiring obedience to tradition.

# Cadence review

The candidates were tested as a spoken set rather than as isolated translations.

## Rhythmic attack and release

- `Kes veth veth` and `Ver jekaen morl` use compact consonantal attack suited to recognition, warning, or finality.
- `Va var ser mahr` and `Vekaen La dren kaveth` release through open vowels and liquids, matching flow and continued burden.
- `Rinaen esh an kel` alternates open vowels with short grounded roots, allowing the phrase to sound elevated without becoming difficult to repeat.
- `Va tal dro; va ver kel` uses balanced parallel commands and a strong call-and-response shape.

## Repetition

`Dar, dar` and `Kes veth veth` use repetition differently. The first repeats an entire verb to regulate conversational speed. The second repeats a noun in separate subject and object roles, creating a memorable mirrored cadence. Neither establishes a general reduplication rule.

## Euphony

No new slang forms or euphonic variants are proposed in the revised Pass 3. Idiom frames preserve their ordinary word boundaries; no phrase-level smoothing is proposed.

# Risks and unresolved decisions

## 1. Social provenance

The revised slang usages have proposed social birthplaces, but those origins remain creator-facing hypotheses rather than canon. Before integration, confirm whether the communities named in Pass 3 feel culturally true.

## 2. Currency over time

Slang changes faster than ordinary vocabulary. Future metadata should be able to distinguish current, dated, revived, occupational, and cross-generational usage. This report does not invent those historical layers prematurely.

## 3. Colloquial grammar boundaries

`Ilin-ka` and `Inko-ka` are proposed ellipses: speakers omit an understood possessed noun, but `-ka` keeps its established linking or possessive function. `Ver-ilin` goes further by proposing a fused register-marked form. It must not be treated as ordinary standard negation without explicit approval, and it needs careful review because its most obvious social function is exclusion.

## 4. Smoke-family concentration

`Mura` appears in several idioms because smoke naturally supports obscurity, rumor, measurement failure, and superficial repair. The uses remain distinct, but the batch should not automatically generate additional smoke expressions. Scene testing should confirm that this is a believable productive metaphor rather than authorial repetition.

## 5. Register and offense

None of the proposed slang usages is a protected-class slur, but `Vekmor` can be cruel and `Felnor` can trivialize real trauma. Their social boundaries are part of the proposal, not optional decoration.

## 6. Source-ambiguous slurs

The eight inherited nation-based slurs are in scope for the Expressions build because slurs are socially marked expressive vocabulary. Inclusion does not resolve or erase their `Source Ambiguity` status. They remain cross-linked dictionary entries and must display a clear offensive-language warning, the group targeted, source provenance, usage context, and unresolved review status. No new slurs are proposed by this decision.

## 7. Proverbs remain deferred

Full proverbs should be tied to attested histories, occupations, famous events, oral traditions, or named anonymous traditions. Inventing them in the same pass would blur everyday conventional speech with cultural wisdom literature.

# Recommended approval structure

The proposal is designed for selective approval. Existing canonical expressive material is a build-content decision rather than a new lexical approval batch:

1. **Established Expressions collection:** canonical interjections, expressive discourse particles and responses, oaths, greetings/farewells, idioms/proverbs, and all eight inherited national slurs. The phrasebank is excluded.
2. **Living Celan 1A — Elevated metaphorical expressions:** candidates 1–12.
3. **Living Celan 1B — Contemporary Ohnoshan idioms:** candidates C1–C10.
4. **Living Celan 1C — Clipped and reanalyzed colloquial forms:** candidates 13–20.
